You Paid Good Money for That Lead. It Got One Call and One Text.

Humans give up on leads way too early. Not because they're lazy — because they're human. Here's what that costs you, and what "never giving up" actually looks like when a machine does the chasing and your closers do the closing.

John Roberts
Sales & F&I Manager, House of Carz · Founder, LotLink

Go pull the invoice from your lead provider this month. AutoTrader, CarGurus, Cars.com, Facebook — doesn't matter which. You know the number. Every one of those leads cost you real money before anyone ever spoke to the customer.

Now do the uncomfortable part. Pull ten of those leads out of your CRM from last month and count the actual touches. Not the automated "thanks for your inquiry" blast — the real attempts. A call. A text. A voicemail that says something.

If you're like most stores I've seen — and like my store was, before I built what I'm about to describe — the average lead got one call and one text. Maybe a voicemail if the salesperson was feeling thorough. If it didn't answer back, it got a status change to "cold" and a one-way ticket to the bottom of the list. You'd be lucky — genuinely lucky — to find much more than that on the average lead handled by the average salesperson.

You paid for thirty days of that customer's attention. You used about ninety seconds of it.

It's not laziness. It's gravity.

I want to be fair to salespeople here, because I've been on that floor. The salesperson who gave your lead one call isn't bad at their job. They're doing the rational thing with the hours they have.

A customer standing on your lot beats a three-day-old internet lead every single time. A fresh lead beats a stale one. A customer who replies beats one who doesn't. So every day, your team triages — and follow-up loses the triage every day, to something that feels more alive. Day 3 becomes day 10 becomes never. Nobody decided to abandon the lead. It just lost every coin flip against something closer.

That's not a discipline problem you can fix with a sales meeting. I tried the sales meetings. It's gravity, and gravity doesn't care about your pep talk.

Meanwhile, the customer is still buying

Here's the part that should keep you up at night: the lead your CRM marked cold on day 3 is still shopping on day 19. Buying a car takes people weeks. They're waiting on a paycheck, a tax refund, a co-signer, a spouse, their own nerve. The question was never whether they'd buy. It was who would still be talking to them when they were ready.

Usually that's whichever store happened to touch them last. Persistence doesn't just protect your lead spend — it quietly takes deals from the store down the road that gave up on day 2. Their one-call-one-text is your day-19 appointment. We've watched a "cold" lead walk in after eleven touches and nine days of silence — the deal existed the whole time; somebody just had to still be there when it woke up.

What "never giving up" actually looks like

This is why we built Jordan — the AI BDC that runs my store and a growing number of others. Not because AI is magic. Because a machine is immune to the gravity.

Jordan answers a new lead in about 30 seconds, at 9 PM on a Sunday same as 9 AM on a Tuesday. And then — this is the part no human can match — Jordan simply never gets tired of the lead:

The cadence keeps going. Texts, emails, and calls, spaced out over days and weeks, each one written for that customer and the car they asked about. Not blast templates. Day 12 gets the same care as minute one.

When the car they wanted sells, the lead doesn't die with it. Most systems go silent the day the car leaves the lot — the customer just never hears from anyone again. Jordan tells them it sold, once, honestly — and pivots to the closest matches in your inventory without missing a step.

When they no-show, Jordan works the reschedule. And when they text "something came up, I'll have to reschedule," Jordan releases the slot, keeps the conversation warm, and pins down the new time. No reminder texts for appointments that no longer exist.

When the bank says "co-signer," Jordan doesn't hear "dead deal." That's a buyer with one obstacle. Jordan coaches them through bringing the co-signer in, and flags your team that a hot one needs a human.

When a lead goes truly quiet, it isn't deleted — it's parked. Weeks later, Jordan circles back. You'd be surprised how many "dead" leads answer the day-45 message, because their timing finally lined up.

And before you ask: persistence is not pestering. Every text lives inside legal calling and texting windows, quiet hours are respected, and one "STOP" ends it instantly and permanently. Polite, spaced, professional — the follow-up you'd do yourself if you had nothing else to do. That's the whole point. Jordan has nothing else to do.

Your salespeople, doing the part they're actually great at

None of this replaces your closers. It feeds them — and honestly, it frees them.

Remember the gravity problem? The customer on the lot beats the follow-up list every time. With Jordan working the list, that's no longer a problem — it's the correct move. Your salesperson can give the customer in front of them their full attention, because nothing is being dropped behind their back. No pile of 40 overdue CRM tasks glaring at them. No "I'll call them tonight" that never happens. No guilt at the back of their mind while they're mid-handshake. The follow-ups are getting worked — every lead, every day, 100% of the time — whether your floor is slammed or short-handed or it's Sunday night.

That's peace of mind you can't get from a to-do list, and it changes how the floor feels.

Sell what's in front of you. The machine holds the line behind you.

And the moment a lead raises their hand — asks for pictures, mentions financing, books a time — your rep gets a text with the context, and takes it from there. Humans close. The machine just refuses to let the lead die before a human gets the chance.

You already paid for the lead. The only question is whether you're going to use the whole thing.
